AI for Teachers: A Calm Place to Start

You do not need to be a tech person to use AI well in a classroom. Start with the tasks around teaching, like planning, parent notes and differentiating a handout, and keep student information out of it.

Step by step

  1. Check your school policy. Ask your principal or district what tools are allowed and what rules apply.
  2. Start with planning. Ask for a lesson outline, a warm-up, or a set of discussion questions.
  3. Give context. State the grade, subject, time available and standards.
  4. Review everything. You know your students. Fix errors and adjust to your class.
  5. Never enter student names or records. Describe a situation without identifying details.

What to expect

Expect time savings on planning and communication.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not enter student data. Avoid skipping district policy.

A good next step

Pick one task to speed up this week.

Where teachers use it most

  • Drafting lesson plans, warm-ups and exit tickets.
  • Making differentiated versions of a reading or worksheet.
  • Writing emails to families.
  • Creating rubrics, quiz questions and practice problems.
  • Brainstorming project ideas.

Your first afternoon, step by step

  1. Pick one lesson you teach soon. Tell the assistant the grade, subject, standard and time.
  2. Ask for three options. "Give me three ways to open this lesson."
  3. Ask for a simpler and a harder version. This is often the biggest time-saver.
  4. Edit and adapt. Keep what fits your students and delete the rest.
  5. Save the prompt. Keep a document of prompts that worked.

Rules of thumb

  • Never paste student names, grades or personal details.
  • Follow your school or district policy on AI.
  • Check facts, dates and quotes.
  • Use AI for a draft, then bring your judgment as the teacher.

A week of small wins

  1. Monday. Ask for three hooks for your next lesson and use the one you like.
  2. Tuesday. Paste a reading and ask for a version one grade lower and one a grade higher.
  3. Wednesday. Ask for a short quiz and an answer key. Check every answer.
  4. Thursday. Draft a family email in two tones and pick one.
  5. Friday. Ask for a reflection list: what worked, what to change.

Prompts to try

  • "Create a 10-minute warm-up for 6th grade science on the water cycle with three questions."
  • "Rewrite this paragraph for students reading two years below grade level without changing the key ideas."
  • "Give me a rubric for a one-page persuasive essay with four levels."
  • "Suggest three ways to differentiate this activity for students who finish early."

Using AI responsibly in school

  • Know your district's policy and follow it.
  • Do not enter student names or private data.
  • Tell students when you use it for materials, if your school expects that.
  • Teach students to verify AI answers and to cite their use of it.
  • Treat AI output as a draft that you review.

Saving time without losing your voice

  • Keep a document of your favorite prompts.
  • Edit AI drafts to include your jokes, routines and examples.
  • Use AI for the first 70 percent and you for the last 30.
  • Take the time saved for planning with colleagues or for students who need you.

A prompt you can copy

Type this into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini

I teach grade [x] [subject]. Draft a 45 minute lesson outline on [topic] with a 5 minute warm-up, a short mini-lesson, an activity for pairs, and an exit ticket. Keep the language suitable for [age].

Good to know. Many districts have AI rules. Following them protects you and your students.

Common questions

Is AI cheating for teachers?

Using a tool to draft materials is like using a template. You remain the professional who checks and teaches.

Can I paste student work?

Only if your district allows it and with names removed. When unsure, do not.

Is it allowed at my school?

Policies vary. Check your district's guidance before using it with student data or materials.

Can it grade for me?

It can help draft feedback comments, but a teacher should review every grade.

Which tool should I use?

Start with any free assistant and try a second one if you want to compare.

Will students notice?

They notice engagement. Materials that reflect your class tend to work best.

Can I use it to write comments on student work?

It can suggest wording, but you should read the work and decide.
